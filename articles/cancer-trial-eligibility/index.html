Broadening Eligibility in Cancer Clinical Trials: Washout, Meds, and Performance Status

An analysis of the FDA's crucial push to modernize oncology trial eligibility criteria, aiming to improve diversity, expedite enrollment, and reflect real-world patient demographics.

1. The Problem with "Perfect" Patients

For decades, oncology clinical trials have relied on highly restrictive inclusion and exclusion criteria. Sponsors designed these protocols to isolate the specific effect of an investigational drug and strictly protect patient safety. The ideal clinical trial candidate was essentially a "perfect" patient: relatively young, possessing perfect kidney and liver function, free of any prior malignancies, taking no complex concomitant medications, and exhibiting excellent physical mobility.

However, this practice systematically excludes a vast portion of the real-world cancer population. Cancer is fundamentally a disease of aging, and real patients frequently present with hypertension, mild organ dysfunction, diabetes, or a history of previous treatments. As a result, the "homogenous" cohorts in clinical trials often fail to represent the very patients who will actually receive the drug post-approval, leading to a profound lack of generalizability regarding the drug's true efficacy and safety profile.

2. The FDA's Modernization Mandate

To address this problem, the FDA and groups such as the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and Friends of Cancer Research have recommended broader, scientifically justified eligibility criteria. FDA guidance documents address specific topics, including organ dysfunction, prior or concurrent malignancies, performance status, and washout periods or concomitant medications. These recommendations are guidance rather than blanket requirements: eligibility criteria should reflect the investigational therapy, trial objectives, available safety data, and the patients who may ultimately receive the treatment.

  • Washout Periods and Concomitant Medications: Blanket washout periods and exclusions for concomitant medications should be scientifically justified rather than copied automatically from older protocols. The appropriate interval depends on the prior therapy, its pharmacology, potential overlapping toxicities, and the trial context; a fixed number of half-lives is not suitable for every treatment.
  • Performance Status: Rather than automatically excluding patients outside ECOG 0–1, sponsors should consider whether a wider performance-status range is appropriate. Inclusion of patients with ECOG 2 or worse depends on factors such as the therapy's expected risks and benefits, disease setting, available safety information, and the trial's objectives; it is not a universal requirement.
  • Organ Dysfunction and Comorbidities: Renal, hepatic, or cardiac impairment and prior or concurrent malignancies should be assessed using criteria appropriate to the specific trial. Controlled comorbidities or stable brain metastases do not automatically make a patient eligible, and broader inclusion still requires an assessment of foreseeable risks, pharmacology, and the ability to interpret safety and efficacy.

3. Accelerating Accrual and Improving Real-World Evidence (RWE)

Broadening eligibility criteria may make trials accessible to more patients and improve how well results apply to the population treated in practice. The effect on enrollment speed, cost, and the interpretability of safety or efficacy results varies by trial; broader criteria should be evidence-based and should not compromise participant safety.

Broadening eligibility can improve representation, but it does not automatically ensure representative enrollment or access to an investigational therapy. Sponsors should track who is screened, excluded, enrolled, and lost to follow-up, and assess whether eligibility changes affect safety monitoring, recruitment, and the applicability of results to the intended treatment population.

Toolkit Tip: Avoid treating a broadened-eligibility cohort and a historical restricted cohort as if they were randomized groups. Differences in disease burden, prior therapy, supportive care, calendar time, and assessment practices may confound comparisons. A Chi-Square Calculator can compare categorical counts under its assumptions, but a p-value alone does not resolve confounding or establish that an eligibility change caused a difference in response or toxicity.
