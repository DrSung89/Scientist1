menu_book Guide to One-Sample, Independent, and Paired T-Tests

Select the test that matches the study design: a one-sample test compares one sample with a specified reference mean, independent-samples tests compare unrelated groups, and the paired test analyzes within-pair changes for linked observations.

One-Sample T-Test

A one-sample t-test evaluates whether the mean of one numeric sample differs from a hypothesized population mean μ₀. Enter at least two independent observations and the reference mean. The reported 95% confidence interval estimates the population mean; the two-sided test evaluates H₀: μ = μ₀. The method assumes independent observations and, especially for small samples, a population distribution without severe skew or influential outliers.

One-Sample T-Statistic:

$$ t = \frac{\overline{x} - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{n}}, \qquad df = n - 1 $$

The effect size shown is signed Cohen's d = (sample mean − μ₀) / sample SD.

1. Understanding the F-Test for Variances

Student's t-test assumes equal population variances. Welch's t-test does not require that assumption and is generally a sound default for comparing two independent means, including when sample sizes or variances differ.

The calculator reports an F-test for equality of variances as a diagnostic only. This test is sensitive to departures from normality, and choosing Student's or Welch's test based on a preliminary variance test can distort the overall error rate. Prefer Welch's test as the default unless your analysis plan justifies the equal-variance assumption independently.

2. Student's T-Test vs. Welch's T-Test

Student's T-Test

Uses a pooled variance and assumes equal population variances. Use it when that assumption is justified by the study design or analysis plan—not simply because a preliminary F-test is not significant.

Welch's T-Test

Does not assume equal population variances and adjusts the degrees of freedom (df) using the Welch–Satterthwaite approximation. It is a robust default for two independent groups.

Welch's T-Statistic Equation:

$$ t = \frac{\overline{X}_1 - \overline{X}_2}{\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{N_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{N_2}}} $$

3. Paired T-Test

A paired t-test first calculates one difference per matched pair, then tests whether the mean difference is zero. Its degrees of freedom are the number of pairs minus one. Pairs must be defined by the study design, and differences from different pairs should be independent; with small samples, the distribution of differences should be reasonably compatible with the t-test model.

Paired T-Statistic:

$$ t = \frac{\overline{D}}{s_D / \sqrt{n}} $$

$D$ is the within-pair difference (A − B); $s_D$ is its sample standard deviation.

4. How to Interpret Your Output Metrics

Beyond the simple P-value, high-impact journals require comprehensive statistical reporting. Our tool provides these essential metrics:

  • P-value: Compare the P-value with the significance level specified in your analysis plan (often α = 0.05). If P is below α, reject the null hypothesis of equal population means under the test assumptions. Statistical significance alone does not establish biological or clinical importance.
  • 95% Confidence Interval (CI): This interval is produced by a method that, over repeated samples under its assumptions, would cover the true population mean difference in 95% of such intervals. For the corresponding two-sided test at α = 0.05, an interval that includes zero indicates that the difference is not statistically significant at that level.
  • Effect size: For independent groups, the signed Cohen's d uses the pooled standard deviation; for paired samples, the signed Cohen's dz uses the standard deviation of within-pair differences. Both signs follow A − B, and the values are not interchangeable. Context matters when interpreting standardized effects.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

When should I choose the paired design?

Choose paired / repeated measurements when each value in A is meaningfully linked to the value in the same position in B, such as before-and-after measurements on the same subject or matched pairs. The test analyzes the within-pair differences; enter each pair in the same order. Use independent samples for unrelated observations. Pairing must come from the study design, not from sorting values to create matches.

What should I do if my sample size is very small (N < 5)?

There is no universal sample-size cutoff that automatically determines whether a t-test is appropriate. With very small groups, assess the data-generating process, independence, outliers, and whether the sampling distribution is plausibly compatible with the model; normality tests have little ability to detect departures at tiny sample sizes. Welch's test addresses unequal variances, not severe skewness or influential outliers. The Mann–Whitney U test is not simply a safer test of the difference in means: it evaluates rank/distributional differences and can be interpreted as a location shift only under additional assumptions. Choose the method based on the study question and design, and report the limitations of a small sample.

Why did my degrees of freedom (df) output as a decimal?

If you look at the Welch's T-Test result, the df is often a fractional number (e.g., df = 14.3). This is normal and correct. It is the result of the Satterthwaite equation mathematically penalizing the test due to the unequal variances between your two groups.
